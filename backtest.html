4. Correlation detail — strongest relationships per result

Analysis

Top 5 per target by |r| (n ≥ 8). r = Pearson correlation; p = nominal significance; adj. p = recomputed with an autocorrelation-adjusted sample size (trending series carry fewer independent observations than quarters). "Holds up" requires n ≥ 10 and adj. p < 0.05. Nothing here is a trading signal on its own.

5. Out-of-sample skill — would using the indicator have helped?

Analysis

Leave-one-out test: for each quarter, pick the best indicator using only the other quarters, predict the held-out quarter, and compare its error to two naive baselines. Skill > 0 means lower error than the baseline.

7. Methodology & caveats (read these)

Analysis
  • Targets (12 quarters): GPI's same-store new-vehicle units and GPU, same-store used retail units and GPU (all YoY %), and adjusted diluted EPS YoY %, taken from each 8-K Exhibit 99.1 (data/gpi-history.json; each release's printed prior-year EPS reconciles to the earlier year's reported EPS). Quarter-over-quarter "changes" tests use the change in each YoY series.
  • Indicators: domestic/imported/total SAAR, auto-loan rate (level and YoY change), gas price, Texas unemployment, CPI used and new (all FRED, long history); Manheim wholesale index; Cox retail listing prices; Ford truck deliveries. Lags tested: same quarter, and indicator one quarter earlier ("1Q lead").
  • Small sample: with n = 12, |r| must exceed about 0.58 to be nominally significant at 5%. A correlation of 0.6 or 0.7 with this few points is weak evidence, not strong.
  • Many comparisons: the grid tests hundreds of indicator × result × lag combinations; roughly 5% would look "significant" by chance alone. The counts are shown in Section 3.
  • Trending series: YoY growth rates drift smoothly (e.g. GPI's new-vehicle GPU recovered steadily from -24% to -5% while the wholesale index also climbed). Such series correlate strongly without any causal link, which is why the adjusted p-value and the out-of-sample test matter more than the raw r.
  • Same-store base changes every year: GPI's "same-store" set is redefined annually, so YoY figures from different quarters are not on a common base. For the same reason unit-count seasonality (Q2→Q3 ratios) was not used as a benchmark; only GPU and EPS were.
  • One-off events inside the window (the mid-2024 CDK dealer-software outage, tariff pull-forward buying in early 2025, GPI's 2026 rebranding disruption, portfolio acquisitions/disposals) are not modelled and can swamp any indicator relationship.
  • Leave-one-out is generous: it uses future quarters to predict past ones, which a real forecaster could not do. A strict walk-forward test would be even harsher and would have too few points to run.
  • Data quality: Manheim 2023 monthly YoY and Cox 2024 retail listing prices were parsed from page text and could not be independently validated (later periods were cross-checked and mostly agreed); series that could not be parsed reliably were excluded rather than patched. Section 6 lists what was and wasn't used.
  • Street consensus comes from vendor articles (Zacks, Benzinga, FXEmpire) surfaced by web search — six quarters only, vendors disagree on both the estimate and the EPS definition. It is context, not an input to any correlation.
  • Not a forecast: the purpose is to see what the indicators can and cannot say. A short sample means "we don't have enough history to say this reliably" is the correct answer for most of the metrics that matter.

Reproducibility

Everything on this page is computed by scripts/backtest.py from data/fred-series.json, data/gpi-history.json, data/indicator-history.json, data/manual-data.json and data/forecast-log.json, and written to data/backtest.json. Re-run it any time; the page re-renders from that file.
